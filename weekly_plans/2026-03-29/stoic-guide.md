# Which Port Are You Sailing Toward?

*Direction* · March 29 – April 4, 2026

---

> "If one does not know to which port one is sailing, no wind is favorable."
>
> — Seneca, *Moral Letters to Lucilius* 71.3

---

## Introduction

The cherry blossoms are out at the Tidal Basin. You stood among them on Sunday — petals drifting, crowds murmuring, your daughter craning her neck toward branches heavy with pink. It was beautiful, and it was already ending. That is the mood of this week: beauty in motion, life asking you where you’re headed.

This week your family considers a new home. You’ll sit in a house that isn’t yours yet and imagine a future there. You’ll pack bags for Cleveland and leave one place for another. These are acts of direction — choosing which port to sail toward, as Seneca put it. The Stoics knew that without a clear aim, even favorable winds are wasted. But they also knew that the aim need not be a destination on a map. The truest direction is internal: Who am I becoming? What kind of life am I building?

Direction doesn’t mean certainty. It means intention. The cherry blossoms don’t know when they’ll fall, but they open anyway — fully, without reservation. That’s the practice this week: open fully toward what matters, even when the future is uncertain.

## Meditations


### 1. The Petals at the Basin · Sunday

You saw the cherry blossoms today. Thousands of people came to see something that will be gone in days. That’s not sad — it’s clarifying. When something is brief, you pay attention. You notice. Direction begins with noticing: where you are, what you have, what is already beautiful and already leaving.

**Prompt:** What in your life right now is beautiful because it won’t last? Write it down. One thing.


> "Think of yourself as dead. You have lived your life. Now, take what’s left and live it properly."
> — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 7.56



### 2. The Question the House Asks · Monday

This afternoon you’ll walk through a house and imagine your family inside it. Rooms will feel right or wrong. A yard will seem big enough or too small. But the real question isn’t about the house — it’s about the life you want to build. Every big decision is an act of direction. The Stoics didn’t say “have no preferences.” They said: know what you’re choosing, and why.

**Prompt:** Before the house visit, each person writes one sentence: “The most important thing about where we live is _____.” Compare notes at dinner.


> "Let all your efforts be directed to something, let it keep that end in view."
> — Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 52.3



### 3. The Current Beneath · Tuesday

Your daughter is in the pool tonight, learning to trust the water. Swimming is an act of direction through resistance — you move forward not by fighting the current but by working with it. Epictetus taught that we waste energy struggling against what we can’t control. Real direction means putting your effort where it counts: your stroke, your breath, your form.

**Prompt:** Where in your life are you fighting the current instead of swimming with it? Name one thing you could stop resisting.


> "First say to yourself what you would be; and then do what you have to do."
> — Epictetus, Discourses 3.23.1



### 4. The Art of the Simple Day · Wednesday

Pizza night. No agenda. The Stoics would approve — not because they valued laziness, but because they understood that an uncluttered day reveals what you actually care about. When there’s nowhere you have to be, where do you drift? That drift is data. It shows you what your internal compass points toward when no one else is steering.

**Prompt:** At the end of today, notice what you did when you didn’t have to do anything. Was it toward connection? Solitude? Play? Creation? That’s a clue about your direction.



### 5. The Open Field · Thursday

A free day in the middle of spring break is a rare gift. Marcus Aurelius warned against filling time with busyness that has no purpose. Today, try the opposite: choose one thing deliberately. Not from obligation. Not from habit. From intention. One meal, one walk, one conversation — chosen because it matters.

**Prompt:** Choose one activity today purely because it aligns with what you value. Before you begin, say out loud or silently: “I am choosing this.”


> "It is not that we have a short space of time, but that we waste much of it."
> — Seneca, On the Shortness of Life 1.3



### 6. What You Carry · Friday

You’re flying to Cleveland today, which means packing — deciding what comes with you and what stays behind. Travel is a physical metaphor for direction: you literally choose what to carry. The Stoics practiced this as a discipline of mind. What thoughts, worries, and expectations are you packing? Which ones can you leave at home?

**Prompt:** As you pack, notice one worry or expectation you’re carrying into the trip. Write it on a scrap of paper and leave it on your nightstand. You can pick it up when you get back — if you still want it.


> "We suffer more in imagination than in reality."
> — Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 13.4



### 7. Arrival Is Not the Point · Saturday

You’re in Cleveland. A different city, different beds, different rhythms. But here’s the Stoic insight: arrival is never the point. The port Seneca spoke of isn’t a place you reach — it’s a way you travel. You can be lost in your own living room and perfectly oriented in a strange city, if your inner compass is calibrated. Direction is not where you are. It’s who you are, wherever you are.

**Prompt:** In this new place, take five minutes alone. Close your eyes. Ask: “Am I pointed toward what matters?” Let the answer come without forcing it.

## Family Exercise: The Compass Question

At one meal this week — any meal — go around the table and each person answers: “What is one thing that matters to you more now than it did a year ago?” No right answers. No judgment. Just listen. Then notice: are your compasses pointing in similar directions, or different ones? Both are fine. The point is to know.

*Seneca believed that most people drift through life without examining their aims. This exercise is a family version of his challenge: name your port.*

---

## For the Young Stoic: The Map in Your Head

Imagine you have a treasure map, but instead of gold, the treasure is the best day you can think of. What’s on it? Who’s there? What are you doing? Now here’s the secret: you don’t need the map to be perfect. You just need to know which direction to walk. If your best day has your family in it, walk toward your family. If it has swimming in it, keep swimming. You already know your direction — you just have to trust it.

*The Stoics said that having a direction — even a wobbly one — is better than wandering. You don’t have to have everything figured out. You just have to know what matters to you, and take one step that way.*
