# The Things That Are Up to Us

*Acceptance* · April 12 – 18, 2026

---

> "Some things are within our power, while others are not. Within our power are opinion, motivation, desire, aversion — in short, whatever is our own doing. Not within our power are our body, our property, our reputation, our office — whatever is not our own doing."
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1.1

---

## Introduction

This week, spring is at full bloom in Reston — the dogwoods don’t worry whether anyone will stop to admire them. They simply flower. There is a lesson in that effortless acceptance, and it arrives at a moment when your family could use it most.

You have made your offer on a house. You prepared thoughtfully, you chose well, you acted with conviction. And now the decision rests with someone else entirely. This is the oldest lesson the Stoics teach: the sharp, liberating line between what is ours to control and what is not. Epictetus opens his entire handbook with it. Marcus Aurelius returned to it again and again while governing an empire. It is not resignation — it is redirection. Pour your energy into your choices, your character, your presence with the people you love. Let the rest arrive as it will.

This week’s seven meditations walk that line together — from recognizing the boundary, to practicing release, to discovering that the deepest freedom lives on our side of the divide.

---

## Meditations


### 1. Drawing the Line · Sunday

Epictetus begins his teaching with an inventory: what belongs to me, and what does not? Our opinions, our desires, our efforts — these are ours. The weather, other people’s decisions, the housing market — these are not. Most of our suffering comes from confusing the two columns. Today, as the week opens, simply notice where your attention lands. Are you rehearsing a conversation with a seller you’ll never have? Are you refreshing an inbox for news that will arrive on its own schedule? The noticing itself is the first act of freedom.

**Prompt:** Draw two columns on a page: “Mine” and “Not Mine.” List everything on your mind this week in the correct column. Circle the items in “Mine” — that’s your real to-do list.


> "Make the best use of what is in your power, and take the rest as it happens."
> — Epictetus, Discourses 1.1



### 2. The Archer’s Release · Monday

The Stoics loved the metaphor of the archer. You can choose your bow, train your arm, aim with care, and release with skill. But once the arrow leaves your hand, a gust of wind, a shift in the target — these are beyond you. The archer’s virtue is in the preparation and the release, never in the landing. You have released your arrow on the house. The wind is not yours to command. Your job now is to stand steady, breathe, and trust that you aimed well.

**Prompt:** Think of one decision you made recently where you prepared well. Write down three things you did right in the preparation — regardless of how it turned out.


> "If you are distressed by anything external, the pain is not due to the thing itself, but to your estimate of it; and this you have the power to revoke at any moment."
> — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 8.47



### 3. Steady Under the Pager · Tuesday

On-call weeks are a masterclass in the dichotomy of control. You do not choose when the alert fires. You do not choose the severity of the incident. What you choose is your response: calm or frantic, methodical or scattered, kind to yourself or punishing. Marcus Aurelius governed an empire while battling plagues and border wars. He did not control when crises arrived. He controlled how he met them. Your on-call rotation is a smaller arena, but the practice is identical.

**Prompt:** Before your on-call shift begins, write a short intention: “When the pager sounds, I will _____.” Fill in one thing within your control — a breath, a pause, a posture.


> "You have power over your mind — not outside events. Realize this, and you will find strength."
> — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 6.8



### 4. The Weather Inside · Wednesday

It is 80°F and sunny outside, and spring is doing exactly what spring does without asking permission. Inside us, the weather is more variable. Anxiety about the house, fatigue from on-call, excitement about Friday’s guests — these fronts collide. Seneca reminds us that we suffer more in imagination than in reality. The house news has not arrived. The on-call incident may never come. The guests will be wonderful. Right now, in this actual moment, the sun is shining. Can you let it in?

**Prompt:** Set three alarms today labeled “Weather Check.” When each sounds, pause and ask: “What is actually happening right now?” versus “What am I imagining?”


> "We suffer more often in imagination than in reality."
> — Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 13.4



### 5. Loving What Happens · Thursday

The Stoics practiced amor fati — love of fate. Not passive acceptance, but active embrace. Whatever answer comes about the house, it is the next chapter, not the last one. Nietzsche later borrowed this idea: to want nothing to be different, not forward, not backward, not in all eternity. This is not about pretending disappointment doesn’t sting. It is about trusting that you can build a good life from whatever materials fate hands you. The dogwood blooms whether you planted it or not.

**Prompt:** Write a short letter to your future self, one week from now. Begin with: “Whatever happened with the house, I want you to know…”


> "Fate leads the willing, and drags along the reluctant."
> — Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 107.11



### 6. The Table You Set · Friday

Guests arrive tonight. Hospitality is one of the purest expressions of what is within our control. You cannot control whether the conversation flows or the timing works perfectly. But you can control the warmth of your welcome, the care in your cooking, the attention you give each person. Marcus Aurelius wrote: accept the things to which fate binds you, and love the people with whom fate brings you together, but do so with all your heart. Tonight, love with all your heart.

**Prompt:** Before your guests arrive, take five minutes alone. Set one intention for the evening that is entirely within your control — perhaps: “I will listen more than I speak.”


> "Accept the things to which fate binds you, and love the people with whom fate brings you together, but do so with all your heart."
> — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 6.39



### 7. The Garden Within · Saturday

A week of practice draws to a close. You have drawn the line, released the arrow, steadied yourself under pressure, checked your inner weather, embraced uncertainty, and welcomed others with open hands. The Stoics compared the soul to a garden: we do not control the rain or the frost, but we choose what to plant, how to tend, when to prune. This garden is always yours. No seller, no pager, no twist of fate can take it from you. Tend it well. Everything you need is already growing inside.

**Prompt:** Walk outside and find one thing blooming that nobody planted on purpose. Sit with it for a moment. Then ask yourself: what is blooming in me that I did not plan?


> "Very little is needed to make a happy life; it is all within yourself, in your way of thinking."
> — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 7.67

## Family Exercise: The Two Buckets

Place two buckets or bowls on the kitchen table, labeled ‘Ours’ and ‘Not Ours.’ Each family member writes down three things on their mind this week on separate slips of paper, then places each slip in the correct bucket. Talk about what you notice. Most of our worries live in the ‘Not Ours’ bucket — and that’s okay. Then take the ‘Ours’ slips and discuss one small action each person can take this week on something they actually control.

*This exercise is a hands-on version of Epictetus’s opening lesson in the Enchiridion: sorting the world into what depends on us and what does not. Making it physical — writing it down, placing it in a bucket — helps the whole family internalize the distinction.*

## For the Young Stoic: Elsie’s Arrow

Imagine you’re an archer shooting an arrow at a target. You practiced a lot, you aimed really carefully, and you let the arrow fly. But then a big gust of wind blows it sideways! Is that your fault? Nope! You did everything you could. Can you think of something this week where you tried your best, but the result wasn’t up to you? Draw a picture of your arrow flying through the wind — and write one thing you’re proud of about how you tried.

*You can always control how hard you try and how kind you are. You can’t control everything that happens after. And that’s perfectly okay.*
