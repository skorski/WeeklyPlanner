Babo Pasta — Egg-Yolk Spinach Tagliatelle with Brown Butter and Parm
Italian
The family favorite. Homemade egg-yolk and spinach tagliatelle, dressed in nutty brown butter, finished with finely grated Parmigiano-Reggiano and torn sage. It's a recipe that earns its weekly spot because every member of the household actually asks for it.
00 flour, egg yolks, spinach, unsalted butter, Parmigiano-Reggiano, sage, lemon zest
Make the dough in the morning so it rests. Cut tagliatelle right before swim, cook on return.
BBC Good Food · 4 servings · Prep 45 min · Cook 10 min

Mamma Karen Says

Blanch the spinach, squeeze it dry like it owes you money, then puree it with egg yolks until bright green. Work it into the flour until the dough is smooth, wrap it, and let it rest before you roll thin sheets. Cut tagliatelle, dust it lightly, and boil it only until tender. Brown the butter until it smells nutty and the milk solids go amber, then catch it before it burns. Toss the pasta with brown butter, pasta water, and a snowy handful of parmesan. Serve right away, because fresh pasta waits for nobody and you made something beautiful.

Grilled Cheese and Tomato Soup
American Comfort
The fastest meal of the week — sharp cheddar grilled cheese with mayo-on-the-outside crust, alongside a quick tomato soup built from a can of San Marzanos, sautéed garlic, butter, a splash of cream, and basil. Twenty minutes door to dinner.
sourdough or country bread, sharp cheddar, mayonnaise, canned San Marzano tomatoes, garlic, heavy cream, basil, butter
Mayo on the outside of the bread for an even golden crust. Soup can be made the night before.
Diethood · 4 servings · Prep 10 min · Cook 20 min

Mamma Karen Says

Start the soup first with garlic, tomatoes, broth, and a splash of cream so it can simmer while you build sandwiches. Butter the bread all the way to the corners, honey, dry corners are a character flaw. Use enough cheese to melt, but not so much the sandwich slides around like a bad decision. Cook low and steady until the bread is deep gold and the middle is gooey. Blend the soup smooth, taste for salt, and give it a tiny pinch of sugar if the tomatoes are sharp. Cut the sandwiches into dunkers and dinner is saved.

Mediterranean-leaning American

bone-in pork chops, couscous, carrots, frozen peas, Dijon mustard, lemon, shallot, chicken stock, butter, parsley

EatingWell · 4 servings · Prep 15 min · Cook 20 min

Buttermilk Pancakes with Sausage and Egg-in-the-Center
American Breakfast-for-Dinner
Tall, fluffy buttermilk pancakes, browned breakfast sausage links, and the centerpiece move: a pancake with a sunny egg cooked right into the middle — like an egg-in-a-hole but griddled in batter. Maple syrup, butter, and cold berries on the side.
all-purpose flour, buttermilk, eggs, baking powder, breakfast sausage links, maple syrup, berries, butter
Rest the batter 10 minutes. Cook eggs into the center on lower heat so the white sets while the bottom of the pancake browns.
King Arthur Baking · 4 servings · Prep 15 min · Cook 25 min

Mamma Karen Says

Brown the sausage first so breakfast has a backbone, then keep it warm. Whisk the dry ingredients in one bowl and the buttermilk, eggs, butter, and vanilla in another. Closing day can feel huge to adults and oddly abstract to an 8-year-old: papers, keys, logistics, grown-up excitement. Elsie may understand that this is important, but she will likely feel it most through small sensory moments — where her shoes go, which window she likes, what the first snack tastes like in the new kitchen.

Try giving her one tiny ceremony that belongs to her. It can be five minutes: let her choose the first song played in the house, pick the first family photo spot, or decide where a future reading corner might live. The words can be simple: 'This is a big family day, and your ideas belong in this house too.'

  1. Hand her the key or let her touch the doorknob and say, 'Want to help us officially welcome the house?'
  2. Offer two or three quick choices: first song, first snack spot, first room to wave hello to, or first family photo.
  3. Name what you notice: 'You are really picturing how we could live here. I love seeing your brain make this place ours.'
  4. End with a repeatable line: 'New house, same team.' Use it later when moving details feel chaotic.

Coach the Sleepover Social Weather

A sleepover with two friends is wonderful and also socially complicated. With three children, someone can feel left out fast; with late-night energy, tiny slights can grow dramatic. At 8, Elsie is practicing loyalty, fairness, and how to stay herself in a group.

Before the fun peaks, try a light side-by-side check-in while setting out pajamas or gimbap plates. Keep it casual, not like a lecture. You might say, 'Three-person games can get tricky. If someone feels left out, what is one kind move you can try?' This plants a social tool before she needs it.

  1. Ask one low-pressure planning question before guests arrive or while food is being set out.
  2. Brainstorm two rescue moves together: invite someone to choose the next game, switch partners, or make a three-person job.
  3. If drama appears, start with feelings: 'Oof, it feels bad to be the one not picked.' Then ask, 'What would help everyone rejoin?'
  4. The next morning, praise one specific social choice you saw: 'I noticed you made room for both friends on the couch. That was generous hosting.'

Turn Swim Butterflies Into a Brave Script

Starting swim team gives Elsie a fresh arena for competence: new coaches, new kids, new rules, and the body-in-motion courage of getting in the water when people are watching. Even active children can feel self-conscious when a familiar skill becomes a team skill.

Try helping her build a tiny script she can carry into the pool. Not a pep talk — just a phrase she chooses. Something like, 'First lap, then I know more,' or 'I can be new and still belong.' When children help choose the words, the encouragement feels like theirs instead of something pasted on by adults.

  1. On the drive or while packing her swim bag, ask, 'Do you want a brave sentence for tonight?'
  2. Offer a few options and let her edit them: 'I can try one thing at a time,' 'New things get easier,' or 'My body knows how to learn.'
  3. After practice, ask for one detail instead of a full report: 'What is one thing you know now that you did not know before practice?'
  4. Celebrate effort specifically: 'You got in, listened, and tried. That is how teammates are made.'

The Mystery of the Three White Stars

The mystery began on rainy Sunday with three white paper stars and no explanation. Chelsie found them tucked under Sharkie's flipper on the kitchen table while rain stitched silver lines down the windows. Beside the stars was a map of Washington, DC, with tiny circles around Arlington National Cemetery, Constitution Avenue, and the long black wall of the Vietnam Veterans Memorial. At the bottom, someone had written: Find who remembers. Mr. Bugles sprang up on Chelsie's left hand, wearing one of her old swim goggles like a helmet. "I DO NOT TRUST MYSTERY STARS," he announced. "THEY ARE TOO POINTY." Mertle, the turtle sock puppet in Chelsie's jacket pocket, raised one stubby arm. "Stars are mostly hot gas at a respectful distance. These appear to be paper. Safer, but less luminous." Chelsie turned the map over. Nothing. She sniffed the paper. It smelled faintly like tomato sauce and flour, because Daddio had been making grandma pizza dough nearby. "A clue," Chelsie whispered. The first circle led only as far as the front porch, where their neighbor Eric was setting a folded flag into a dry spot under the awning. Eric was active in the US Army, and Chelsie knew he did scientific research, though she imagined that meant beakers wearing tiny helmets. "Excuse me," Chelsie said. "Do you know why someone gave me stars and a map that says find who remembers?" Eric looked at the stars, then at the rainy street. "I might know one piece," he said. "Memorial Day is when our country remembers the service members who died while serving. For people in the military, it is not just a long weekend. It can be a quiet day, because someone may be thinking of a friend, a teammate, or a name they carry in their heart." Chelsie went still. The rain sounded softer after that. "Do you know someone who died?" she asked carefully. Eric did not look upset that she asked. "Some soldiers do. Some don't. Your family is fortunate not to know someone directly who passed in service. That is a blessing. But the day asks all of us to remember families who do." He handed her a tiny silver sticker shaped like a microscope. "My Army work is research. Science can serve too. Sometimes protecting people looks like a lab, not a battlefield." Chelsie put the sticker on the first paper star. The second clue smelled like pine needles and wet sidewalk. It led to Ed, who was trimming a branch that had drooped after the rain. Ed had served in the Army doing forestry things, which sounded to Chelsie like being a forest detective. "Forestry things?" Mr. Bugles said, trying to sound official. "I CAN IDENTIFY ONE TREE: BIG." Ed laughed, but gently. "In the Army, land matters. Trees, trails, maps, camps, erosion, weather. If you do not understand the land, you cannot take care of the people moving across it." Mertle nodded so hard his cotton shell wrinkled. "Terrain is a sentence the Earth writes in hills and roots." "That's very fancy," Chelsie told him. "I contain multitudes," Mertle said. Ed showed Chelsie how rainwater ran along the curb, carrying little helicopters from the maple tree. "Memorial Day events in DC are part of remembering too," he said. "At Arlington, people visit rows and rows of white stones. At the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, a wreath is laid for service members whose names we may not know. That matters. Being unknown does not mean being forgotten." Chelsie pressed one maple seed to the second star and drew a tiny tree beside it. By Monday morning, the rain had become drizzle. Daddio packed the map into the car, along with sandwiches, apples, and a small container of leftover Babo Pasta because Chelsie believed pasta improved most investigations. They watched part of the National Memorial Day Parade near Constitution Avenue, where bands marched, flags lifted in the damp air, and veterans waved from cars. Nobody was silly about it, but nobody was gloomy the whole time either. It felt solemn, which was Chelsie's delicious new word of the day: serious with love inside it. At the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, Chelsie saw people touch names on the black stone. Some left flowers. Some stood without speaking. She did not know any of the names, but she understood they belonged to real people, not just history-book words. "This is a quiet clue," she whispered. Mr. Bugles whispered back, "I AM BEING MY BEST VOLUME." The third clue came later in the week, after swim team started Wednesday with wet hair, kickboards, and Chelsie's arms feeling like noodles. On Thursday, the sun finally came out bright and clean for the new house closing. Everyone was celebrating the milestone, but the white paper star still had no sticker, no seed, no answer. That evening, while Daddio grilled BBQ and the yard smelled like smoke and pepper, Dale stopped by. Dale worked at Amazon now, but he had been deployed to Iraq multiple times protecting the nation. He noticed the blank star in Chelsie's hand. "That's an important little star," he said. "It's the last clue," Chelsie said. "I talked to Eric and Ed. I went to the parade and the memorial wall. But I still don't know who wrote find who remembers." Dale leaned against the deck rail. The sunlight made the grass look new. "When I was deployed, letters and ordinary things mattered. A joke from home. A picture. Knowing people remembered us. But Memorial Day is especially for those who did not come home. It is okay to enjoy your BBQ and your family and your new house. Those good things are part of what people serve to protect. Just make room in the day to be grateful." Chelsie looked at the blank star. "So it should stay blank?" "Maybe," Dale said. "A blank star can stand for people whose stories you do not know yet." After dinner, Daddio brought out the last clue: a small envelope that had been hidden under the pizza peel. Inside was a note in his handwriting. Dear Investigator, it said, Memorial Day is not a mystery to solve once. It is a remembering we practice. I made the map so you could ask real people real questions. Chelsie smiled because the mystery had not been hidden treasure. It had been hidden conversations. She taped the three stars beside the map: Eric's microscope star, Ed's maple-seed star, and Dale's blank star. Then she added one more tiny circle around Arlington and one around the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier. Mertle performed a small shimmy in the sunset. Mr. Bugles saluted with a swim goggle, then whispered, "THANK YOU," which, for him, was almost ceremonial. Chelsie decided that remembering was not the same as being sad all day. Remembering was saying names when you knew them, honoring sacrifices when you did not, and noticing how lucky it was to sit at a table with people you loved while the week turned from rain to sun.
